#### Conceptual Questions

1. **A request-duration histogram exports 8 finite buckets, one +Inf bucket, a sum and a count for every label combination. The service has 100 instances, 20 endpoints and 4 status values. Calculate the number of series. Then a developer adds a user_id label with 50,000 distinct values: calculate the worst-case series count, explain which resources of a time-series database are hurt, and list three techniques that keep cardinality under control and where each one is applied.**

   Each label combination produces 8 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 11 series: nine bucket series, one sum and one count. There are 100 × 20 × 4 = 8,000 combinations of instance, endpoint and status. Therefore, the histogram produces 8,000 × 11 = 88,000 series, counting exactly the series specified in the question. Adding 50,000 distinct user_id values creates a worst case of 88,000 × 50,000 = 4,400,000,000 series, or 4.4 billion. This assumes every user appears in every original label combination; the actual count depends on the combinations observed.

   This cardinality increase consumes memory for active-series metadata, labels and open chunks; enlarges the label index and posting lists; increases write-ahead-log traffic, disk storage and compaction work; and increases ingestion CPU and network traffic. Queries may need to scan and aggregate many more series, raising query latency, memory and CPU usage. Frequently appearing and disappearing label values also create series churn, so even short-lived series are expensive.

   Three techniques are: 

   First, at application instrumentation, use bounded labels such as route templates and status classes instead of user IDs, request IDs or raw URLs; keep individual identifiers in logs or traces. 

   Second, at scrape or collector ingestion, reject unwanted metrics or drop high-cardinality series, and explicitly aggregate away unnecessary dimensions when the metric semantics allow it. 

   Third, at the backend or tenant boundary, enforce active-series and new-series quotas, monitor cardinality growth and notify the owning team. 

2. **Instance A served 800 requests with a 99th percentile of 20 ms and instance B served 200 requests with a 99th percentile of 600 ms. Explain why the average of the two percentiles is not the 99th percentile of the combined traffic. Then compare client-side summaries, fixed-bucket histograms and sketches by whether they can be aggregated across instances, their accuracy, and their cost, and explain why bucket boundaries should be chosen around the latency objective.**

   The ordinary average is (20 + 600) / 2 = **310 ms**, while a request-count-weighted average is (800 × 20 + 200 × 600) / 1,000 = **136 ms**. Neither calculation gives the combined p99. A percentile identifies a rank in an entire distribution, not an additive quantity. The combined traffic has 1,000 observations, and its p99 is determined by observations near the 990th position after sorting, depending on the quantile convention. The two local p99 values do not reveal the rest of either distribution, so the exact combined p99 cannot be calculated from the information provided. For example, if all 800 requests on A took 20 ms and all 200 on B took 600 ms, the reported local percentiles would fit the question, but the combined p99 would be 600 ms.

   A client-side summary computes quantiles inside each application instance and exports the selected quantile values, usually together with a sum and count. Its exported quantiles cannot be merged into an overall quantile across instances. The sum and count can still be aggregated to calculate an overall mean. Quantile accuracy depends on the summary algorithm and configured error tolerance, and the application pays CPU and memory costs to maintain its local distribution. Export cost includes one series per configured quantile plus the sum and count.

   A fixed-bucket histogram exports counts for predefined bucket boundaries. Histograms with compatible boundaries can be aggregated by summing corresponding buckets before estimating a percentile. Accuracy depends on how narrowly the buckets bracket the desired quantile, because the precise positions of observations within a bucket are unknown. Wider buckets provide less precise estimates. Each bucket adds a time series per label combination, so adding buckets improves resolution but increases ingestion, storage and query costs.

   A mergeable sketch, such as t-digest or DDSketch, stores an approximate distribution that can be combined across instances using the algorithm's merge operation. Its memory and accuracy tradeoff is configurable, but guarantees depend on the algorithm: DDSketch is designed for relative value error, while t-digest emphasizes accuracy near the tails without the same universal relative-error guarantee. Sketches are compact compared with retaining every observation, but require computation and compatible ingestion, storage and query support. Bucket boundaries should be concentrated around the latency objective so the system can distinguish acceptable from unacceptable latency with useful resolution. A boundary exactly at an objective such as 300 ms also allows the fraction of requests at or below that threshold to be calculated directly from the bucket count rather than estimated through percentile interpolation.

3. **A tail-based sampling tier receives 2,000 traces per second, each with 8 spans of 400 bytes on average, and holds each trace for a 20 second decision window. Calculate the memory needed to buffer the spans. Explain why all spans of a trace must be routed to the same sampler node, and compare routing by hash(trace id) modulo N with consistent hashing when the tier grows from 4 to 5 nodes, including the fraction of traces that move in each case.**

   Each trace contains 8 × 400 = 3,200 bytes of span data. The incoming rate is 2,000 × 3,200 = 6,400,000 bytes per second. Buffering 20 seconds therefore requires **128,000,000 bytes**, or **128 MB in decimal units, approximately 122.1 MiB**. This represents 40,000 traces containing 320,000 spans across the tier. It is a span-payload estimate, not a complete memory allocation: trace indexes, object overhead, queues, decision caches, traffic bursts and operational headroom require additional capacity. With perfectly balanced traffic, the raw payload averages 32 MB per node across four nodes, or 25.6 MB per node across five.

   Tail sampling waits to observe a trace before deciding whether to keep it, often using conditions such as errors or high latency. All spans for a trace must reach the same sampler node so that it can evaluate those conditions consistently and retain or drop the trace together. Splitting a trace across independent samplers can produce incomplete decisions and fragmented stored traces.

   With hash(trace id) modulo N, changing N from 4 to 5 changes the destination for approximately **80% of traces**. Assuming stable node numbers and uniformly distributed hashes, only four of the twenty possible hash residues modulo 20 have the same result under modulo 4 and modulo 5, so 20% stay and 80% move. Balanced consistent hashing moves approximately 1/5 = 20% of traces to the new node, leaving about 80% with their previous owners. Virtual nodes help distribute ownership evenly, so the observed fraction may differ slightly from the ideal. Consistent hashing reduces disruption but does not move buffered state automatically; traces already in flight can still split during the decision window unless routing changes are coordinated, old ownership is retained temporarily, or state is transferred.

4. **The header traceparent: 00-4bf92f3577b34da6a3ce929d0e0e4736-00f067aa0ba902b7-00 arrives at a service. State the meaning and size of each of the four fields. What does the last field tell the service to do, how does it keep the sampling decision consistent across all services of a trace, and what does the service put in the parent-id field of the header it sends to the next service?**

   The first field, 00, is the trace-context version: one byte represented by two hexadecimal characters. The second field, 4bf92f3577b34da6a3ce929d0e0e4736, is the trace ID: 16 bytes, or 128 bits, represented by 32 hexadecimal characters. It identifies the distributed trace. The third field, 00f067aa0ba902b7, is the parent ID: eight bytes, or 64 bits, represented by 16 hexadecimal characters. It identifies the upstream span associated with the incoming request. The fourth field, 00, contains trace flags: one byte represented by two hexadecimal characters. These byte sizes describe decoded values; the HTTP header carries their hexadecimal text.

   The least significant flag bit is the sampled flag. Here it is unset, so a service using the tutorial's parent-based sampling policy honors the upstream unsampled decision and normally does not export sampled spans. It still propagates context. Carrying the same decision downstream avoids independent sampling choices that would fragment the trace. Strictly, the flag communicates the upstream decision rather than imposing an unconditional requirement on every tracing implementation. For an instrumented outgoing call, the trace ID remains unchanged and parent-id becomes the span ID of the service's outgoing client span, which is the next service's parent. It does not reuse the incoming parent ID for a newly created outgoing span.

5. **A service has a 99.95% availability objective measured over 30 days. Calculate the error budget in minutes. For burn rates of 14.4 and 6, calculate the error ratio that triggers the alert and the fraction of the budget consumed after 1 hour and after 6 hours respectively, and the number of days after which the budget would be exhausted at burn rate 6. Explain why a good alert uses a long window and a short window together, and which of the two alerts should page someone and which should create a ticket.**

   The allowed error fraction is 1 - 0.9995 = 0.0005, or 0.05%. Thirty days contain 30 × 24 × 60 = 43,200 minutes, so the error budget is 43,200 × 0.0005 = 21.6 minutes of equivalent complete unavailability. For a request-based availability SLI, the actual budget is 0.05% of eligible requests; translating it into minutes assumes a time-based measure or equivalent uniform traffic.

   The error-ratio threshold is burn rate × allowed error fraction. At burn rate 14.4, the threshold is 14.4 × 0.0005 = 0.0072 = 0.72%. Sustaining that rate for one hour consumes 14.4 × 1 / 720 = 0.02 = 2% of the 30-day budget, equivalent to 0.432 minutes. At burn rate 6, the threshold is 6 × 0.0005 = 0.003 = 0.30%. Sustaining it for six hours consumes 6 × 6 / 720 = 0.05 = 5% of the budget, equivalent to 1.08 minutes. Starting with the full budget, a constant burn rate of 6 exhausts it in 30 / 6 = 5 days. These time-fraction calculations assume a constant traffic rate when the SLI is request-based; with variable traffic, actual budget consumption depends on the failed-request count.

   A good alert requires both a long window and a short window to exceed the same burn threshold. The long window confirms sustained impact and reduces sensitivity to brief spikes, while the short window confirms that the issue is still happening and allows the alert to clear promptly after recovery. Example pairs are one hour and five minutes for burn rate 14.4, and six hours and thirty minutes for burn rate 6. In the homework's two-level fast-versus-slow alert scheme, the 14.4 alert should page the on-call engineer, while the lower-severity 6 alert should create a ticket for follow-up. This severity assignment is a policy choice, not a consequence of the arithmetic; losing 5% of the budget in six hours may also justify paging for a critical service.

6. **Explain how a telemetry pipeline is designed so that a backend outage never blocks or slows the monitored application, what the overload options are at each stage (buffering, dropping, sampling, rate limiting) and the tradeoff of each, and the difference between at-least-once and at-most-once delivery for telemetry. Then explain why the alerting path should run in a separate failure domain from the systems it monitors and what a watchdog (dead-man's switch) alert detects.**

   The application should not wait synchronously for a telemetry backend or a durable acknowledgement on the request path. Instead, it records telemetry into a bounded queue using a non-blocking offer, while a separate exporter batches and sends data with timeouts and bounded retries. If the queue is full, the application drops telemetry according to policy, increments a drop counter and continues serving requests. The aim is to prevent a backend outage from propagating blocking or unbounded resource use into the application, rather than to promise that telemetry has literally zero overhead. Exporter CPU, memory and network use must also be limited so background work cannot starve request processing.

   At the application or local-agent stage, a bounded memory buffer absorbs short bursts cheaply, but queued data can be lost on a crash. A capped disk spool provides longer outage tolerance, but adds disk I/O and needs a strict capacity limit so it cannot fill the application's disk. Sampling or filtering at the source reduces downstream cost immediately but permanently loses detail. At the collector or gateway stage, bounded queues, memory limits, per-tenant rate limits and priority-based dropping prevent one producer from exhausting shared resources. Head sampling is relatively cheap, while tail sampling can retain interesting traces but requires its own bounded state. Rejection or throttling must be handled by the exporter rather than making application threads wait.

   A durable broker between collection and storage can retain data during backend outages and let storage consumers recover at their own pace. Its tradeoffs are infrastructure cost, latency, possible duplicate delivery and finite retention. If the outage lasts longer than available capacity, the system must shed data, expire old data or reject new ingestion rather than buffer indefinitely. At storage ingestion, quotas and rate limits protect disks, indexes and active-series memory; rejected data must be retried within bounds or dropped by upstream stages. Buffering trades memory or disk for temporary tolerance, dropping prioritizes availability over completeness, sampling trades fidelity for lower volume, and rate limiting enforces resource fairness but may lose data without adequate buffering. These choices should be observable through independent health and drop metrics.

   At-most-once delivery means a record arrives zero or one times: failures can lose it, but the delivery mechanism does not retry in a way that creates duplicates. At-least-once delivery retries until acknowledgement and can resend a record if the receiver stored it but its acknowledgement was lost. Consumers must tolerate or deduplicate duplicates. An at-least-once guarantee only holds within the durability and retention assumptions of that stage; dropping data when an application queue fills means the entire pipeline is not lossless end to end. A common design accepts loss at the application edge to preserve application availability and uses durable, at-least-once delivery between downstream stages.

   The alert evaluation and notification path should run in a separate failure domain, such as a separate cluster, account or region, so the outage being monitored does not also disable its alarms. Its network, authentication and notification dependencies should not rely entirely on the failing production system. A watchdog, or dead-man's switch, sends a continuously firing heartbeat alert to an independent external receiver. That receiver alerts when heartbeats stop arriving within the expected interval. This detects a broken monitoring or notification path, including failed evaluation, routing or delivery, even when the monitoring system cannot report its own failure. It verifies that the heartbeat path is alive, not that every metric or alert rule is correct.
