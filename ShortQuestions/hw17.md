# Java Observability Homework: Questions and Answers

## 1. Histogram cardinality

Question

A request-duration histogram exports 8 finite buckets, one +Inf bucket, a sum and a count for every label combination. The service has 100 instances, 20 endpoints and 4 status values. Calculate the number of series. Then a developer adds a user_id label with 50,000 distinct values: calculate the worst-case series count, explain which resources of a time-series database are hurt, and list three techniques that keep cardinality under control and where each one is applied.

Answer

There are 9 bucket series per label combination (8 finite buckets plus +Inf), plus `_sum` and `_count`, for 11 series per combination.

- Label combinations: 100 instances × 20 endpoints × 4 status values = 8,000.
- Series before `user_id`: 8,000 × 11 = 88,000 time series.
- With 50,000 distinct user IDs: 8,000 × 50,000 × 11 = 4,400,000,000 time series in the worst case.

This multiplication assumes every user ID occurs with every instance, endpoint, and status combination. In practice, the actual number may be lower, but the upper bound is enormous.

High cardinality increases the number of active series and the size of the series index, label metadata, in-memory head, and storage. It also increases ingestion, compaction, query, and network costs, and can cause memory pressure or slow queries.

Three controls, applied at different points:

1. Instrumentation design: Do not use unbounded identifiers such as user IDs, request IDs, or raw URLs as metric labels. Use bounded categories instead, such as a route template (`/users/{id}`) or a small user tier.
2. SDK or collector processing: Drop or rewrite risky labels before export. For example, remove `user_id` from metrics or map arbitrary status text to a bounded set of values.
3. Backend limits and monitoring: Configure per-metric or per-tenant active-series limits, reject or quarantine excessive series, and alert on cardinality growth. This is a last line of defense; it does not replace good instrumentation.

## 2. Aggregating latency percentiles

Question

Instance A served 800 requests with a 99th percentile of 20 ms and instance B served 200 requests with a 99th percentile of 600 ms. Explain why the average of the two percentiles is not the 99th percentile of the combined traffic. Then compare client-side summaries, fixed-bucket histograms and sketches by whether they can be aggregated across instances, their accuracy, and their cost, and explain why bucket boundaries should be chosen around the latency objective.

Answer

The average of the instance percentiles is (20 ms + 600 ms) / 2 = 310 ms, but that is not the combined p99. Percentiles describe positions in distributions; averaging two percentile values does not combine the underlying requests. The instances also handled different traffic volumes (800 versus 200). To calculate the exact combined p99, we need the underlying latency distributions or mergeable distribution data; these two p99 values alone are not enough.

| Method | Aggregatable across instances? | Accuracy | Typical cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Client-side summary with locally computed quantiles | Usually no. A percentile such as p99 cannot generally be merged into an exact global p99. | Accurate for the local window, subject to the summary's quantile algorithm and sample size. | Low network/storage cost; state and computation live in each client. |
| Fixed-bucket histogram | Yes, if every instance uses the same bucket boundaries. Bucket counts can be summed, then a percentile can be estimated from the combined counts. | Approximate; precision depends on bucket widths, especially near the requested quantile. | Moderate series and storage cost; predictable and straightforward to query. |
| Mergeable quantile sketch (for example, a DDSketch or compatible sketch) | Yes, when the sketch implementation supports merging compatible sketches. | Approximate with a configured error guarantee; the type of guarantee varies by sketch. | Usually more client CPU and memory than simple counters, with compact transmitted state compared with very fine histograms. |

Choose histogram boundaries around the service's latency objective and the ranges operators need to investigate. For example, include boundaries just below and above an SLO threshold such as 300 ms. This makes the share of requests meeting the objective visible and improves percentile estimates near that threshold. Buckets that are too wide hide useful changes; too many buckets increase series and storage cost.

## 3. Tail-based trace sampling

Question

A tail-based sampling tier receives 2,000 traces per second, each with 8 spans of 400 bytes on average, and holds each trace for a 20 second decision window. Calculate the memory needed to buffer the spans. Explain why all spans of a trace must be routed to the same sampler node, and compare routing by hash(trace id) modulo N with consistent hashing when the tier grows from 4 to 5 nodes, including the fraction of traces that move in each case.

Answer

The span arrival rate is 2,000 traces/s × 8 spans/trace = 16,000 spans/s. Over a 20-second decision window, the sampler holds 320,000 spans.

- Buffered span payload: 320,000 × 400 bytes = 128,000,000 bytes, or 128 MB (about 122 MiB).
- This is a lower-bound estimate. Span objects, indexes, trace metadata, allocator overhead, and safety headroom increase actual memory use.

All spans belonging to a trace must reach the same sampler so it can evaluate the complete trace (for example, whether any span has an error or unusually high latency) before deciding to keep or discard it. Splitting a trace across samplers can lead to incomplete decisions and broken trace assembly.

When the pool grows from 4 to 5 nodes:

- Hash(trace ID) modulo N: Changing the modulus remaps most keys. With an even hash distribution, about 1/5 remain on the same node and 4/5, or 80%, move.
- Consistent hashing:A new node takes roughly its share of the ring, so approximately 1/5, or 20%, move to the new node, assuming balanced virtual nodes and traffic. The exact fraction varies with the ring and key distribution.

Consistent hashing reduces redistribution during scaling, though production systems should also account for uneven traffic and use virtual nodes or another balancing method.

## 4. W3C Trace Context

Question

The header `traceparent: 00-4bf92f3577b34da6a3ce929d0e0e4736-00f067aa0ba902b7-00` arrives at a service. State the meaning and size of each of the four fields. What does the last field tell the service to do, how does it keep the sampling decision consistent across all services of a trace, and what does the service put in the parent-id field of the header it sends to the next service?

Answer

The W3C `traceparent` value has four hyphen-separated fields:

| Field | Value | Size and meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Version | `00` | 2 hexadecimal characters (1 byte); identifies the header format version. |
| Trace ID | `4bf92f3577b34da6a3ce929d0e0e4736` | 32 hexadecimal characters (16 bytes); identifies the whole distributed trace. |
| Parent ID | `00f067aa0ba902b7` | 16 hexadecimal characters (8 bytes); identifies the caller's span, which is the parent of this service's span. |
| Trace flags | `00` | 2 hexadecimal characters (1 byte); carries flags, including the sampled bit. |

Here the sampled bit is unset, so the incoming context indicates that the trace is not sampled. A service's tracing policy determines how to act on that context; with a parent-based policy, it normally does not record/export the trace as sampled. The service propagates the sampling decision in the outgoing trace flags so downstream services make a consistent decision. A service that starts or continues a trace should create its own span ID and put that new ID in the outgoing header's parent-id field. It keeps the same trace ID.

## 5. Availability SLO and burn-rate alerts

Question

A service has a 99.95% availability objective measured over 30 days. Calculate the error budget in minutes. For burn rates of 14.4 and 6, calculate the error ratio that triggers the alert and the fraction of the budget consumed after 1 hour and after 6 hours respectively, and the number of days after which the budget would be exhausted at burn rate 6. Explain why a good alert uses a long window and a short window together, and which of the two alerts should page someone and which should create a ticket.

Answer

A 99.95% availability objective permits a 0.05% error ratio.

- 30 days = 43,200 minutes.
- Error budget = 43,200 × 0.0005 =21.6 minutes of unavailability.

A burn rate is the observed error ratio divided by the allowed error ratio. Thus, the error ratio that corresponds to a burn rate is burn rate × 0.05%.

| Burn rate and window | Triggering error ratio | Budget consumed in that window |
|---|---:|---:|
| 14.4× over 1 hour | 14.4 × 0.05% = 0.72% | 14.4 × 1 / (30 × 24) = 2%|
| 6× over 6 hours | 6 × 0.05% = 0.30% | 6 × 6 / (30 × 24) = 5% |

At a sustained 6× burn rate, the budget is exhausted in 30 days / 6 = 5 days.

A long window confirms that the burn is sustained and prevents brief spikes from causing unnecessary alerts. A short window makes the alert respond promptly when a serious incident begins. Requiring both windows to exceed the threshold gives a faster signal without paging on a momentary blip.

For standard SRE multi-window burn-rate alerting, both 14.4× over 1 hour and 6× over 6 hours are generally page-worthy: they consume 2% and 5% of the budget, respectively, in relatively short periods. If a team deliberately reserves paging for the faster threshold and treats the slower one as lower priority, the 14.4× alert should page and the 6× alert can create a ticket, but that policy risks delaying response to a sustained 6× burn.

## 6. Telemetry pipeline reliability

Question

Explain how a telemetry pipeline is designed so that a backend outage never blocks or slows the monitored application, what the overload options are at each stage (buffering, dropping, sampling, rate limiting) and the tradeoff of each, and the difference between at-least-once and at-most-once delivery for telemetry. Then explain why the alerting path should run in a separate failure domain from the systems it monitors and what a watchdog (dead-man's switch) alert detects.

Answer

The application should record telemetry through low-overhead instrumentation and hand it to a bounded, asynchronous queue. Export happens on background workers with strict timeouts, bounded retries, and backoff. A local agent or collector can batch and forward data, optionally using a bounded disk queue for brief backend outages. Exporter queues, retries, and disk usage must all have limits: telemetry must never wait indefinitely on a remote backend or consume unbounded application resources. If the backend remains unavailable and the buffers fill, the system sheds telemetry according to its chosen policy while the application continues serving requests.

Overload controls and tradeoffs:

- Buffering: Absorbs short bursts or outages and smooths traffic. It costs memory or disk and only postpones loss; a full buffer must eventually drop or reject data.
- Dropping: Protects application and collector capacity by discarding telemetry, often oldest, newest, or lowest-priority data. It reduces observability during overload.
- Sampling: Sends only a subset of events, traces, or spans. It reduces volume while retaining representative data, but can miss rare failures unless the policy is designed to retain errors or slow traces.
- Rate limiting: Caps the amount accepted or exported per unit time. It protects downstream systems but rejects excess telemetry and can create blind spots during high-traffic incidents.

At-least-once delivery retries until data is acknowledged, reducing loss but allowing duplicates if an acknowledgement is lost or a retry occurs. Consumers may need deduplication. At-most-once delivery sends without retrying after uncertain failures, avoiding duplicates but potentially losing data. Telemetry pipelines commonly accept some loss or duplication rather than allowing telemetry delivery to slow the monitored application.

The alerting path should be in a separate failure domain (for example, a separate account, region, or independently operated monitoring service). If the monitored service, its network, or its telemetry backend fails, an alerting system that shares the same failure can disappear at the same time. A watchdog or dead-man's switch expects a regular heartbeat; it alerts when that heartbeat stops, detecting that the monitoring or reporting path may have failed even if no normal alert was delivered.
